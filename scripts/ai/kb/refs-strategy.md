# Reference index — strategy, planning and positional play

@ nimzowitsch-my-system | Aron Nimzowitsch | My System | 1925 | strategy,middlegame,endgame |
- Open files: the rook's goal is to penetrate along an open file to the 7th/8th rank; occupy the file first, then the entry square.
- The 7th rank: a rook (or two) there attacks pawns from the side and confines the king; "the absolute seventh" ties the enemy king to the back rank.
- The passed pawn has a "lust to expand": it must be blockaded, ideally by a knight on the square directly in front of it.
- The blockader is safe from frontal attack, gains activity, and the blockade itself paralyses the pawn's supporting pieces.
- Overprotection: defend a strategically important point (a strong centre pawn, an outpost) more times than it is attacked, so the defending pieces become strong.
- Prophylaxis: before carrying out your plan, prevent the opponent's freeing moves and plans.
- Exchanging: exchange the opponent's defenders of key squares; time exchanges to win tempi.
- Restraint, blockade, destruction: the three stages of fighting an enemy pawn majority or mobile centre.
- The centre can be controlled by pieces rather than occupied by pawns (hypermodern view).
- Pawn chains: attack the chain at its base; the base is the weakest link.
- The isolated pawn: blockade it, exchange pieces, then attack it in the endgame.
- Elastic manoeuvring against weaknesses: shift the attack between two weaknesses on different flanks.

@ nimzowitsch-chess-praxis | Aron Nimzowitsch | Chess Praxis | 1936 | strategy,games |
- Illustrates My System through annotated games: prophylaxis, blockade and overprotection in practice.
- The "mysterious rook move": placing a rook on a closed file in anticipation of it opening later.
- Restraining the opponent's pawn levers (e.g. keeping ...d5 or ...f5 under control) before attacking.
- Manoeuvring behind one's own lines to regroup pieces to better squares when the position is closed.

@ nimzowitsch-blockade | Aron Nimzowitsch | Blockade | 1925 | strategy |
- The blockade as a strategic method: stopping pawns by placing a piece in front of them.
- Best blockaders are knights; bishops and the king can blockade too, especially in endgames.
- A blockaded pawn becomes a target and the squares around it become weak.

@ capablanca-fundamentals | José Raúl Capablanca | Chess Fundamentals | 1921 | endgame,middlegame,opening |
- Study endings first: they teach the power of the pieces and the importance of the king.
- Basic mates and the king's active role; the opposition in king and pawn endings.
- When ahead in material, exchange pieces (not pawns) and simplify into a won ending.
- Develop knights before bishops; control the centre; don't move the same piece twice without reason.
- The "hole": a square that can no longer be defended by pawns is a permanent outpost.
- A plan must be based on the features of the position; even a bad plan is better than none.
- In the middlegame, combine attack with simplification when ahead.

@ capablanca-primer | José Raúl Capablanca | A Primer of Chess | 1935 | endgame,opening,middlegame |
- Introductory course from the basic mates to simple endings and opening principles.
- Emphasises clarity, piece activity and the king's activity in endgames.

@ capablanca-last-lectures | José Raúl Capablanca | Last Lectures | 1966 | strategy,endgame |
- Lectures on general principles: the centre, mobility of pieces, pawn majorities and endings.
- The queenside majority: mobilise it to create a distant passed pawn in the endgame.
- Simple, harmonious positions are easier to play; avoid unnecessary complications.

@ capablanca-my-career | José Raúl Capablanca | My Chess Career | 1920 | games |
- Capablanca's early games with notes: clarity of plans, endgame technique, efficient simplification.

@ lasker-manual | Emanuel Lasker | Lasker's Manual of Chess | 1925 | strategy,endgame,opening |
- Chess as a struggle: the value of pieces depends on the position; "economy" of effort.
- Steinitz's theory explained: attack only when you have an advantage; otherwise accumulate small advantages.
- The principle of the least valuable defender; pieces should co-operate.
- Endgame studies showing the king's power and zugzwang.

@ lasker-common-sense | Emanuel Lasker | Common Sense in Chess | 1896 | opening,middlegame,endgame |
- Opening rules: develop quickly, don't make pawn moves without purpose, castle early, don't bring the queen out early.
- Attack where you are stronger; defend economically.
- Practical endgame rules: activate the king, create passed pawns.

@ steinitz-modern-instructor | Wilhelm Steinitz | The Modern Chess Instructor | 1889 | strategy,opening |
- The founding statement of positional chess: accumulate small advantages (better pawn structure, bishops, space, weak squares).
- An attack is only justified when there is an advantage; the side with the advantage must attack or lose it.
- The king is a strong piece that can defend itself; premature attacks fail against correct defence.
- The bishop pair as a lasting advantage in open positions.

@ tarrasch-game-of-chess | Siegbert Tarrasch | The Game of Chess | 1931 | strategy,opening,endgame |
- Mobility and space: a cramped position carries the germ of defeat.
- Rooks belong behind passed pawns — both your own and the opponent's.
- Open lines for the pieces; the importance of development and the centre in the opening.
- The isolated d-pawn is acceptable in exchange for free piece play (Tarrasch Defence ideas).

@ tarrasch-300-games | Siegbert Tarrasch | Three Hundred Chess Games | 1895 | games,strategy |

- "Knights on the rim" and badly placed pieces as long-term weaknesses.

@ reti-modern-ideas | Richard Réti | Modern Ideas in Chess | 1923 | strategy,opening,history |

- Hypermodern opening idea: invite the opponent to build a pawn centre, then attack it with pieces and pawns.
- Morphy's lesson: in open positions, development and open lines decide.

@ kotov-think-gm | Alexander Kotov | Think Like a Grandmaster | 1971 | calculation,strategy,training |

@ shereshevsky-endgame-strategy | Mikhail Shereshevsky | Endgame Strategy | 1981 | endgame,strategy |

@ silman-reassess | Jeremy Silman | How to Reassess Your Chess (4th ed.) | 2010 | strategy,middlegame |

- Don't react to the opponent's threats only; form your own plan from the imbalances.
- Bishop vs knight, good vs bad bishop, space and how to use it, weak squares as homes for knights.
- Target weaknesses that cannot be defended easily; the opponent's worst piece should be kept bad.
